# Hydro Excavator

## Summary

A hydro excavator digs with high-pressure water (or, on units with an onboard air compressor, an air knife) and vacuums the loosened soil into a debris body. It is used for potholing, daylighting and exposing underground utilities, trenching, and digging near live gas, electrical, fiber and water lines, where it is the standard non-destructive method. It breaks down compacted ground, clay, roots, gravel, frozen and rocky soil that an industrial vacuum cannot. Spoil is normally a wet slurry, and the customer arranges the water supply and disposal. It is the wrong choice for bulk dry material (fly ash pits, grain silos), plant cleanouts or tank cleaning, which suit an industrial vacuum. Hydro excavation trailers suit small jobs and tight access.

### Group A: Hydro Excavator, high level

**Q: Is the spoil from a hydro excavator always wet, or can the unit recover dry material as well?**

Hydro excavation normally produces wet spoil, because the high-pressure water used to cut and loosen the soil liquefies the material and suspends it before vacuum recovery.
The unit can recover dry material, but that is not its primary purpose. If the job requires purely dry spoil, or involves dry bulk material, dust, aggregate, sludge, or industrial cleanout work, an industrial vacuum is the recommended equipment instead.

**Q: Hydro excavators expose infrastructure using water cutting or air knifing. When is air excavation recommended over water excavation, and does that change which unit I get?**

Water excavation is recommended for hard clay, caliche, frozen ground, and high-speed production.
Air excavation is recommended when the job requires immediate on-site backfilling using the dry dug material, or when working around highly sensitive, older utilities where water pressure poses a damage risk. More broadly, air can be preferred when the customer wants to loosen soil without introducing water: for example, when water could create disposal, freezing, contamination, or site-management issues.
Unit impact: yes, the excavation method affects which equipment is appropriate. Choosing air requires a truck equipped with an onboard air compressor system (air knife) rather than just a high-pressure water pump. The application, soil conditions, utility exposure requirements, and disposal plan should all be considered when selecting the unit.

**Q: How is a hydro excavator different from a combination sewer cleaner? Both use high-pressure water plus vacuum recovery. What is the practical difference from the customer's point of view?**

Both use water and vacuum, but they are designed around different applications.
- Combination sewer cleaner: optimized for pipe cleaning, that is, cleaning sewer and storm systems using high-pressure water to break up and flush material while the vacuum recovers the debris. It features a high-flow, lower-pressure water pump feeding a long hose reel designed to travel down lines, alongside a standard vacuum boom.
- Hydro excavator: primarily an excavation and material-removal machine, optimized for vertical and horizontal digging, used for potholing, daylighting utilities, trenching, and exposing underground infrastructure. It features a lower-flow, much higher-pressure water system (up to 4,000+ PSI) utilizing handheld wands and lances, a heavy-duty debris body built for heavy mud and rocks, and a high-strength excavating boom.

**Q: How is a truck-mounted hydro excavator different from a hydro excavation trailer, and when is the trailer the better call?**

A truck-mounted unit is a self-contained work platform that can travel to the jobsite and operate with its own chassis and equipment.
A hydro trailer gives more flexibility when the customer already has a suitable tow vehicle or wants a smaller, more mobile setup, and lets the customer get the excavation equipment without renting an entire truck. It is better for small-scale daylighting (1 to 4 potholes a day), valve box cleaning, smaller jobs, locations where maneuverability matters, or tight urban settings and parking garages where a Class 8 truck cannot fit.
When to choose the trailer: when tow vehicles are readily available and the total debris volume per day is under 500 to 800 gallons.

**Q: What materials or ground conditions can a hydro excavator handle that an industrial vacuum can't?**

A hydro excavator can handle solid, compacted ground, heavy clay, tree roots, gravel, frozen ground, and rocky soil matrices by breaking them down with water. The key difference is that the hydro excavator actively loosens and excavates the ground, while a standard dry industrial vacuum can only recover material that is already loose, uncompacted, pre-broken, or can be picked up directly.

**Q: What jobs is a hydro excavator not the right choice for?**

It is the wrong choice for bulk dry product recovery and large-scale dry material removal (for example, grain silos or fly ash pits), industrial plant cleanouts, tank or silo cleaning, deep structural mine shaft cleaning, or jobs where the primary requirement is moving large quantities of dry bulk material. Those applications are typically better suited to an industrial vacuum. It is also the wrong choice for jobs where adding liquid water creates an environmental or weight-based disposal violation.

**Q: What are the most common reasons customers rent a hydro excavator rather than buying or subcontracting the work?**

- To avoid capital expenditure on highly specialized machinery, along with the maintenance, storage, and operating costs of ownership.
- To scale up for short-term or temporary excavation needs, such as utility mapping and daylighting projects, without committing to a long-term asset.
- To bypass the long lead times and high costs associated with hiring third-party utility subcontractors, allowing the customer's own crews to perform the work rather than subcontracting the excavation.

**Q: What water capacity does a hydro excavator carry, what is the realistic run time before a refill, and who arranges the water supply?**

Typical full-size units carry 1,200 to 1,500 gallons of clean water. Depending on nozzle size and flow rate (GPM), that provides roughly 2 to 4 hours of continuous digging before a refill is needed. Actual run time varies with the specific unit configuration, operating conditions, and water usage.
The customer is responsible for arranging the water source, including securing hydrant permits or staging a support water truck.

**Q: Is a reclaim or recirculation option available on any units in the fleet?**

No. Water reclamation and recirculation systems are highly specialized and generally unavailable on standard hydro excavators in the rental fleet, because the heavy, abrasive solids dug from the ground rapidly accelerate pump wear.

### Group D: Material handling: Hydro Excavator

Framed around what the unit can excavate and what ends up in the spoil tank.

**Q: What is the maximum rock or debris size a hydro excavator can pick up? At what point does material need to be broken up or removed another way?**

Full-size hydro excavators typically use an 8-inch vacuum hose, meaning they can pick up rocks and debris up to approximately 6 to 7 inches in diameter without clogging the boom. Hydro excavators remove loose rock, gravel, and other debris, but oversized material may not pass through the vacuum hose or system.
Once material exceeds that size, or if it consists of heavy, tightly bound consolidated chunks, it must be broken manually with a digging lance or pneumatic tools, removed manually, or removed mechanically before vacuuming. Large rocks, concrete, metal, and roots may need to be handled with another piece of equipment. If significant oversized material is expected, Vac2Go should review the application and equipment configuration before the job.

**Q: How do different soil types affect performance: clay, sand, rocky fill, caliche, hardpan?**

Hydro excavation can handle a broad range of soils, but excavation speed, production rates, and water requirements change substantially with soil conditions.
- Sand and loose soils: the easiest material to excavate; washes away smoothly and vacuums up rapidly.
- Clay: sticky and highly cohesive; requires higher water pressure and specialized rotating nozzle tips to shave off layers without clumping, and takes considerably longer than sand.
- Rocky fill: easily managed as long as individual rocks fit within the vacuum hose diameter, though heavily compacted or rocky fill can take considerably longer.
- Caliche and hardpan: densely compacted, cement-like soils that significantly slow down production. They require high-pressure water combined with targeted, close-range blasting to fracture the structural composition.
- Frozen ground: can take considerably longer than sand and loose soils.

**Q: Can it excavate frozen ground? Is heated water standard, optional, or unavailable?**

Yes, hydro excavators can efficiently excavate frozen ground, and heated water significantly improves performance. Heated water comes from an onboard boiler or water heater, and its rating varies by unit. Hot water capability is a standard expectation for specialized winter builds but is technically an optional feature depending on the specific model rented, and heated-water capability varies by truck, so customers should request a unit equipped with a boiler or heated-water system when working in frozen conditions.

**Q: Can it handle contaminated soil: hydrocarbon-impacted material, remediation spoil? What changes about handling and disposal?**

Yes. Hydro excavators are widely used to clean and recover contaminated soils and can be used for some contaminated-soil and remediation applications. The extraction process itself remains unchanged, but handling and disposal logistics shift entirely.
Contaminated material may require specific PPE, equipment configuration, decontamination procedures, and transportation requirements. The material must be treated as regulated waste and cannot be dumped at standard clean-fill sites: spoil must be transported to a certified environmental disposal or remediation facility, or other approved disposal facility, that can treat wet slurry or hazardous waste. The customer is responsible for accurately identifying the material and following any applicable laws.

**Q: Can it be used to excavate hazardous or regulated material?**

Hydro excavators may be able to excavate certain hazardous or regulated material, but every such job must be reviewed and approved by Vac2Go before the equipment is dispatched. Material type, hazard classification, concentration, site conditions, required equipment configuration, and disposal requirements all have to be assessed case by case. Contact a Vac2Go representative to scope the work rather than assuming a standard unit is suitable.

**Q: What are the limits on working around live electrical, gas, and fiber lines, and what does the customer need to have in place before the unit arrives?**

Hydro excavation is the gold standard for non-destructive digging near live electrical, gas, and fiber optic lines, and is commonly used to expose live utilities including gas, electrical, fiber, water, and telecommunications lines. It is a controlled excavation method, but it is not a substitute for utility locating or safe excavation procedures.
To ensure safety, operators must reduce water pressure, typically to under 2,000 to 2,500 PSI depending on regional regulations, to prevent tearing utility insulation.
Before the unit arrives, the customer must have all applicable utilities located and clearly marked via a certified site utility locate service (811), provide the operator with known utility information, complete a comprehensive hazards site assessment, and follow applicable 811, private locating, site-specific, and utility-owner requirements.

**Q: Can it work near hot infrastructure such as steam lines or hot process areas?**

Yes. Hydro excavation relies on remote-controlled booms and extended vacuum and water lines, so material can be extracted from a distance, keeping personnel completely away from high-heat hazards. That said, live steam lines and hot process equipment require job-specific review: the customer should provide information about the infrastructure, temperatures, process hazards, and site requirements before the equipment is dispatched.

**Q: Can spoil be dewatered on site, or does it leave as slurry, and who handles disposal of wet spoil?**

Hydro excavation normally produces a wet soil and water slurry that is collected in the truck's debris tank. Dewatering capability varies, and customers should not assume that the material can simply be discharged back onto the site.
No, Vac2Go does not advise on or handle spoils or any disposal. Logistics and regulatory compliance for spoil disposal remain the customer's responsibility.

**Q: Are there materials or conditions where a hydro excavator should never be used?**

A hydro excavator should never be used if the pressurized water risks creating a structural sinkhole or destabilizing nearby structural foundations. Avoid it when dealing with unknown, highly reactive chemical compounds, or in tightly enclosed spaces without proper wet/dry filtration ventilation.

### Group E: Sizing and "is this the right truck": Hydro Excavator

**Q: How do I know if I need a full-size hydro excavator vs. a hydro excavation trailer or a smaller unit?**

Choose a trailer-mounted unit if you are working in tight urban alleys, residential areas with low weight allowances, or only require sporadic potholing.

**Q: What drives the sizing decision most often: debris tank capacity, water capacity, boom reach, or dig depth?**

The sizing decision is most frequently driven by a combination of debris tank capacity and water capacity. The limiting factor is how long the truck can stay productive on-site before running out of fresh water or filling its debris tank to its maximum weight or volume limit.

**Q: How deep and how far from the truck can a hydro excavator realistically work?**

Realistically, standard hydro excavators operate highly efficiently at depths of 10 to 20 feet and horizontal distances of 100 to 200 feet from the truck. While high-vacuum blowers (rated up to 27" Hg) can pull material from deeper or further away, efficiency and suction speed drop off significantly as hose length increases.

**Q: For a customer with a daily production target of so many potholes or linear feet per day, how many units do they typically need?**

To calculate the exact number of units a customer needs for a daily production target, specific project variables are required. For instance, digging deep 12"x12" utility potholes in loose sand takes a fraction of the time compared to cutting long, linear trenches through compacted clay.
