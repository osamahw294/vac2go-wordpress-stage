# Liquid Vacuum

## Summary

A liquid vacuum truck recovers and hauls liquids, slurries and pumpable waste, using a vane pump (LVT; LVTS with a stainless tank) or a positive displacement blower (LVPD) for deeper lift and thicker slurry. Typical jobs: septic and grease trap pump-outs, oil-water separators, industrial wastewater and sludge, catch basins and sumps, flood water, drilling mud and brine, and hydro excavation slurry. It handles no dry material. Corrosive liquids need a stainless or lined tank compatible with the product, hazardous liquids need a DOT 407 or DOT 412 code tank, and volatile flammables belong on a liquid ring unit. A tanker carries more over the highway; a liquid vacuum truck is the agile, self-contained choice for pulling material on site.

### Group A: Liquid Vacuum, high level

**Q: What do the LVPD, LVTS, and LVT designations stand for, how do the units differ, and how do I know which one I need?**

- LVPD: Liquid Vacuum with Positive Displacement blower. Features a high-CFM blower capable of deep pulling power and handling thicker slurries. Best used when you have a long vertical lift of liquids and heavy solids.
- LVTS: Liquid Vacuum Truck Stainless. A stainless steel tank with a vane pump system, used for corrosive liquid and light sludgy material, provided the acidic material is compatible with stainless steel SS304 and the hardware on the truck.
- LVT: Liquid Vacuum Truck. A vane pump system, best applied to non-hazardous and non-corrosive liquid and sludgy materials.
- Selection rule: an LVPD when the job involves a long vertical lift of liquids, heavy solids, or thicker slurries; an LVTS when the material is corrosive and compatible with SS304 stainless; an LVT for non-hazardous, non-corrosive liquid and sludgy material.

**Q: Can a liquid vacuum truck handle any dry material at all, or is it strictly liquids and pumpable waste?**

Strictly no dry material. A standard liquid vacuum truck is built for liquids, slurries, and pumpable waste only. These trucks rely on liquid to seal pumps or require fully wet material to prevent damaging the internal tank and filtration systems. Running purely dry, dusty, or granular material through a dedicated liquid vac truck can cause severe mechanical damage or even spark a dangerous dust explosion.

**Q: How is a liquid vacuum truck different from a liquid ring unit, and when does the distinction matter?**

A liquid ring unit is a specialized type of liquid vacuum truck, typically used to handle hazardous and non-hazardous liquids. What separates it is the pump: it is equipped with a liquid ring vacuum pump rather than the traditional rotary vane pump or positive displacement blower used on the LVT and LVPD. A standard liquid vacuum truck uses an air blower or vane pump that can generate heat and sparks, making it unsafe for volatile fumes. A liquid ring unit uses a continuous water seal inside the pump mechanism to compress gas without metal-to-metal contact, completely eliminating ignition sources, which is critical for refinery and explosive vapor applications. The liquid ring unit is also specified as intrinsically safe.

**Q: How is a liquid vacuum truck different from a vacuum tanker? Is a tanker simply a liquid vacuum with a larger barrel, or a different kind of unit?**

They are different kinds of unit. A liquid vacuum truck is a single, self-contained, truck-mounted chassis with its own pump, highly maneuverable on site. A vacuum tanker is a massive semi-trailer barrel pulled by a semi-tractor, built and optimized for bulk, high-volume over-the-road transport rather than tight-access operations, and it needs a semi with a wet kit to operate the vacuum system on the tanker.

**Q: Is the pump on a liquid vacuum truck a positive-displacement blower, a vane pump, or both depending on the unit? Does that difference matter, and which configurations are available?**

Vac2Go stocks three types of pump system: liquid ring pump, positive displacement blower equipped, and vane pump equipped. Positive displacement blowers give deep vacuum pulling power; vane pumps give quick pressure-discharge and suit light liquids. Yes, the difference matters immensely to the customer, because a vane pump cannot pull material from deep vertical depths, whereas a PD blower can.

**Q: What materials can a liquid vacuum handle that an industrial vacuum can't?**

Corrosive, acidic, hazardous and 100% liquid materials. Liquid vacuums are built to handle highly corrosive, volatile, or pure liquid chemicals that would leak out of or ruin the dry filtration baghouses of an industrial air mover.

**Q: What jobs is a liquid vacuum truck not the right choice for?**

A liquid vacuum truck is the wrong choice for any job involving dry powders (including bone-dry gravel or powder transfers), thick unpumpable solids, highly volatile chemicals, dry abrasive sand blasting media, heavy structural excavation, or deep excavations requiring high airflow. Using a liquid truck for these applications can ruin the vacuum pump, violate transportation laws, or trigger catastrophic field accidents.

**Q: What are the most common reasons customers rent a liquid vacuum truck?**

Industrial plant turnarounds and shutdowns, emergency response and environmental cleanup including pond overflows, industrial tank cleanouts, construction site dewatering and excavation support, seasonal municipal projects, commercial waste routing spikes, and commercial grease and wastewater management.

### Group D: Material handling: Liquid Vacuum

**Q: What is the maximum solids content a liquid vacuum can handle before the job needs an industrial vacuum or a liquid ring instead?**

A conventional liquid vacuum truck handles a maximum solids content of 15% to 20%, provided the material remains a flowable, pumpable liquid. Once the waste transitions into a dense slurry, thick cake, or contains heavy abrasive sediment, rotary vane pumps lose efficiency. Anything thicker than that limit requires an LVPD or a liquid ring unit to prevent pump cavitation and line plugging.

**Q: Can it handle sludge containing rags, debris, or grit?**

Yes, a liquid vacuum truck can handle sludge containing rags, debris, and grit. For heavier sludge carrying a greater debris load, a trash pump or industrial vacuum is the preferred choice for the job, since rags can clog the standard 4-inch or 6-inch intake valves of a liquid vacuum.

**Q: Can it handle high-viscosity material: heavy oil, grease, asphalt emulsion, tank bottoms? Is tank heating available?**

A liquid vacuum truck can handle certain high-viscosity materials, including heavy oils and grease, but it struggles or fails entirely if the material is too dense to flow or lacks a liquid carrier. Asphalt emulsions and heavy tank bottoms are too thick to handle without lowering the material's viscosity.
On heating: tank heating is not a standard feature on trucks in the fleet. Heating does help a liquid vacuum truck's performance by lowering viscosity, so contact a Vac2Go representative and the team will see what can be arranged for a specific job.

**Q: Can it handle hot liquids? What are the temperature limits, and what governs them?**

Yes, hot liquids can be handled up to a limit. The maximum temperature for a liquid vacuum truck is 120 F. Higher temperatures risk imploding the vacuum tank seals, boiling the pump seal water, or destroying internal chemical liners.

**Q: Can it handle hazardous or regulated liquids?**

Yes, a liquid vacuum truck can handle hazardous or regulated liquids, provided the truck is a certified DOT 407 or DOT 412 code tank. Not every unit in the fleet is code-certified, and a non-code tank is legally limited to hauling non-hazardous liquids only. Contact a Vac2Go representative about securing a code-certified unit for this type of work.

**Q: Flammable and combustible material is usually routed to liquid ring units. Should a standard liquid vacuum truck never be used on flammables, or is it configuration-dependent?**

It is configuration-dependent, with a hard limit. Vane pumps generate high mechanical friction and heat, and if raw flammable vapors travel directly through a hot vane pump the vapor can ignite inside the pump housing. To safely use a rotary vane or blower truck for flammables it must be outfitted with a Vapor Recovery System, and with spark-arresting exhausts and grounding reels. Standard liquid vacuums should never be used for volatile flammables (Class 1 liquids) without that equipment, and for highly flammable materials a liquid ring truck is legally and operationally required.

**Q: Can it handle acidic or corrosive liquids? Are lined or stainless tank options available in the fleet?**

Only in the right tank. The standard Keith Huber Dominator is constructed from a carbon steel shell, and raw acids or caustics will rapidly eat through carbon steel, ruining the asset and causing a catastrophic chemical leak. Acidic or corrosive liquids can be handled only if you rent a unit equipped with an internal epoxy / FRP liner or a dedicated stainless steel tank. Stainless steel units are available in the fleet, but they cannot handle all acids or caustics, only some types of acids at certain concentrations.

**Q: Can it handle slurry at 100°C / 212°F and above?**

No. A liquid vacuum truck cannot handle slurry at 100 C (212 F). At that temperature the liquid will flash to steam under vacuum, destroying suction capability and ruining pump internals.

**Q: What liquids should never be loaded into a liquid vacuum truck?**

High-vapor-pressure hydrocarbons and unblended gases, unknown volatile solvents without proper grounding, high-temperature boiling liquids, untested or incompatible chemical mixtures, highly reactive acids that clash with the tank metallurgy, shock-sensitive, unstable or un-stabilized explosive solutions, and liquids creating steaming reactions.

**Q: What tank certifications does the liquid vacuum fleet carry: DOT 407, DOT 412, ASME, vacuum-rated vs. non-vacuum?**

The fleet carries DOT 407 (low pressure chemical) and DOT 412 (corrosive waste) certifications in addition to ASME vacuum ratings. ASME "U" or "UM" certifies that the tank's shell thickness (typically 5/16-inch steel on standard units such as the Keith Huber Dominator) and its structural welds can withstand full negative vacuum pressure without buckling or imploding. Not all units carry all certifications, so discuss job specifications with a Vac2Go representative.

### Group E: Sizing and "is this the right truck": Liquid Vacuum

**Q: How do I know if I need a liquid vacuum truck vs. a vacuum tanker vs. a portable pump?**

It comes down to job size, driver qualifications, and space for equipment. Use a portable pump if you are transferring clean water over flat ground between two close ponds. Use a liquid vac truck if you need to suck up heavy sludge from a pit and move it. Use a tanker if you have hundreds of thousands of gallons of liquid to haul over public highways.

**Q: What drives the sizing decision: barrel capacity, pump CFM, or lift and distance?**

Customer needs and job size drive it, and specifically barrel capacity (payload) and pump CFM.

**Q: What is the maximum lift a liquid vacuum can achieve, and how does hose distance affect it?**

A practical maximum vertical lift of 25 feet. On distance, every additional 50 feet of horizontal hose dramatically increases friction loss and reduces the maximum achievable vertical lift; the sweet spot for rapid, efficient pumping is a horizontal run of 100 feet or less.

**Q: For a multi-day liquid removal project, how do you decide between one truck cycling to disposal and a truck supported by tankers?**

The decision balances distance, volume, and operational uptime. If the disposal site is more than 20 to 30 miles away, keep the vacuum truck on site to continuously suck out the pit and discharge directly into staging vacuum tankers that cycle back and forth to disposal. This maximizes on-site labor efficiency.
