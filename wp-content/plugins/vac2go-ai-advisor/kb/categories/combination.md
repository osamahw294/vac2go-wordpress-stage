# Combination

## Summary

A combination unit (combination sewer cleaner, sewer jetter, sewer combo) pairs a high-flow water jetter on a large hose reel with a vacuum system and debris body. It cleans sewer and storm lines, catch basins, culverts and lift stations: the jetter breaks up and flushes material while the vacuum recovers it, including grease (FOG), and roots with cutter nozzles. It is strictly a wet-work machine: no dry material and no hazardous products. Small potholing jobs are possible, but formal daylighting, deep or frozen-ground excavation and air excavation need a hydro excavator. Choose a combo when lines need jetting and the debris must be removed, a jetter-only unit when downstream flow carries the debris away, and a vacuum-only unit for static pits and basins.

### Group A: Combination, high level

**Q: Is a combination sewer cleaner the same thing as a combination sewer jetter?**

A combination sewer jetter and a combination sewer cleaner are both the same type of truck.

**Q: Can a combination unit recover dry material, or is it strictly a wet-work machine?**

Standard combination trucks are strictly wet-work machines. They are used only for semi-wet, wet and liquid material. Running dry material through them bypasses the fluid-separation design and can ruin the blower. For dry material recovery, a dedicated industrial vacuum loader / air-mover must be used.

**Q: How is a combination truck different from a jetter-only trailer?**

The combination truck has a debris tank and a hydro reel, and with a 1 inch hose on the big reel it runs higher water pressure and can produce 80 to 100 GPM of water volume. The trailer unit has a 1/2 inch hose with 30 to 40 GPM output, no debris body, and carries less water onboard.
Advisor note: Unit literature differs from these general figures: the GapVax MC1510 sheet lists 60 to 100 GPM, and the GapVax G7 Trailer Jetter brochure shows a 3/4" reel and 500 or 700 gallon tanks. Use the unit's own figures when a specific unit is discussed.

**Q: Can a combination unit be used for excavation or daylighting? When is a combination unit acceptable for excavation, and when is a hydro excavator required instead?**

Yes, a combo can be used for hydro excavation, but only small jobs or potholing is recommended: very light, shallow excavation, potholing, or clearing soft catch-basin blockages where water pressure demands are minimal. It should not be used for air knifing. Insist on a dedicated hydro excavator for any formal utility daylighting, deep excavation, frozen-ground work, air excavation or large trenching jobs, because combos lack onboard water heaters (boilers) and the debris body and boom geometry needed for continuous structural digging. The difference is primarily tool layout: combination trucks feature a large, mechanized hose reel winding hundreds of feet of heavy-duty sewer hose designed to traverse underground pipelines, alongside a front or rear-mounted catch-basin vacuum boom, while a dedicated hydro excavator is configured exclusively for ground digging, featuring onboard water heaters (boilers) and handheld excavation lances.

**Q: What materials can a combination unit recover that an industrial vacuum can't?**

Liquids, or anything made up of 50% water or more. Combination units excel at recovering highly liquid slurries, heavily emulsified greases, and flushed sewer debris. An industrial vacuum (air mover) can easily become overwhelmed or leak fine mist when handling massive volumes of liquid-dominant stormwater runoff.

**Q: What jobs is a combination unit not the right choice for?**

Dry materials and any hazmat products: dry industrial powder cleanups (such as fly ash or cement dust), hazardous dry chemical recoveries, deep mining excavations, or long-distance dry material transfers.

**Q: What are the most common reasons customers rent a combination unit?**

Municipalities and contractors rent them for routine sanitary sewer line clearing, emergency culvert or storm drain blockages, catch basin maintenance, municipal water line break mitigation, pipe inspections, jetting large pipe, cleaning lift stations, small hydro excavation or potholing jobs, water sump cleanouts, and jobsite material that is liquid, wet to semi-wet and sludgy.

**Q: What pipe diameters and line lengths can it realistically clean? Are there practical minimum and maximum limits?**

Pipe diameters ranging from 4 inches up to 72 inches and above. On line length, the standard hose reel allows clearing line segments up to 600 to 800 feet in a single run.

**Q: For storm drain inspections with a combination unit, is CCTV or camera equipment available, or is inspection limited to visual?**

No. The Vac2Go fleet is focused on high-performance heavy trucks and does not carry CCTV or robotic camera equipment.

**Q: Is a recycler / water reclaim option available in the fleet, and when is it worth it?**

No, there are no recyclers in the fleet. This is not an option.

### Group D: Material handling: Combination

**Q: What debris size can a combination unit vacuum, and does the jetting operation change that?**

The units run an 8-inch vacuum system matching the hydro excavator, allowing up to 6-inch solids, and are equipped with a 12 or 13 yard debris body. Jetting does not degrade the performance of the truck and nothing changes with the jetting capabilities. If anything, jetting helps: it breaks down compacted clods and pushes debris closer to the suction nozzle, making it easier to vacuum.
Advisor note: Debris bodies vary by unit: the GapVax MC1510 is 10 cu yd and the Camel Max comes in 9, 12 and 16 yard versions. Use the unit's own figures when a specific unit is discussed.

**Q: Can it handle heavy grit, sand, and rock in a catch basin, or is there a point where you would send an industrial vacuum or liquid vacuum instead?**

A combo can handle sand, rock and heavy catch basin grit, but rock can sometimes be bigger than the truck can handle and will plug the boom: 5 inch to 6 inch maximum rock diameter. If a basin is packed with compressed, bone-dry sand or massive gravel beds, an industrial vacuum (air mover) or a heavy-duty liquid vacuum truck with a positive-displacement blower is more efficient and helps prevent plugging the water separator filters.

**Q: Can it handle grease and FOG (fats, oils, grease)?**

Yes. The truck handles fats, oils and grease: the high-pressure jetter breaks up grease blankets and the vacuum sucks them up.

**Q: Can it handle roots and solid obstructions? What nozzle or cutter options are available?**

Yes. Roots are handled with specialty mechanical attachments: chain cutters, saw-tooth nozzles, rotating carbide blades, or front cutting with rear thrust style nozzles, all driven by the jetter's water flow.

**Q: Can it handle hazardous or regulated material: contaminated stormwater, industrial sewer discharge?**

Contaminated stormwater and industrial sewer discharge can be handled, provided the truck is designated for wet environmental waste. Standard combination trucks cannot handle highly volatile or corrosive materials, and they cannot haul certified hazardous waste unless the unit carries specialized equipment. For hazardous material the unit would only handle a residual, not a neat hazardous product.

**Q: Can it handle hot process water or industrial discharge? Are there temperature limits?**

Warm industrial discharge can be handled, and the limit depends on seals and gaskets. 140 F (60 C) is the maximum normal operating temperature. Above that, standard vacuum hose seals, pump seals, and internal tank coatings are at risk. Some units may be specified to handle higher temperatures, so discuss anything above 140 F with a Vac2Go representative before the rental.

**Q: Can it handle acidic or corrosive wastewater?**

Standard carbon-steel debris bodies degrade rapidly in acidic or corrosive service, so acidic wastewater requires a stainless steel body variant. Contact Vac2Go for this type of material to confirm compatibility and arrange dispatch of a suitable unit.

**Q: What water pressure and flow do the units run, and what does that mean for pipe material? Can the jetter damage clay, PVC, or lined pipe, and how does the operator avoid that?**

The units typically run water pressures up to 3,000 PSI, with water flows ranging from 0 to 100 GPM depending on the specific pump and nozzle configuration.
Yes, the jetter can damage pipe. Flow and pressure can be adjusted for pipe material: while modern, structurally sound pipes easily handle peak performance, older or fragile materials such as clay, thin cast iron, and orangeburg pipe are highly vulnerable to structural failure or joint separation under maximum pressure. High flow can easily fracture degraded clay pipe or strip thin liners if an improper nozzle is used or if the hose is held static. Operators avoid damage by keeping the nozzle moving and dropping the pressure when working in clay or aged PVC.

**Q: Can water be decanted back into the line on site, and what is the policy on waste left in the truck?**

Decanting water back into a sewer or utility line on site is allowed only if permitted by local environmental and utility regulations. Compliance is the responsibility of the customer. Vac2Go has no place to dispose of waste, so the truck needs to be cleaned out before it is returned.

**Q: Are there materials that should never be run through a combination unit?**

Dry material, hazardous material, acidic or corrosive products, highly concentrated acids, volatile flammable fuels, dry explosive dusts, and unpumpable boulders.

### Group E: Sizing and "is this the right truck": Combination

**Q: How do I know if I need a combination unit vs. a jetter-only or a vacuum-only unit?**

Choose a combo if you are dealing with a pipe that needs to be jetted, or if water and vacuum operations are both part of the task, meaning you must simultaneously clear lines and remove the resulting debris. Choose a jetter-only unit if you are simply flushing lines where downstream flow handles the debris. Choose a vacuum-only unit if you are simply cleaning out a static pit, basin, or tank without the need for water blasting.

**Q: What drives the sizing decision: debris tank capacity, water capacity, jetter pressure and flow, or hose reel length?**

Debris tank capacity and water capacity drive the physical sizing decision. High GPM jetting empties water tanks fast; a 12-yard debris tank paired with a 1,500-gallon water capacity ensures the longest continuous run time. Jetter performance (pressure and flow) and hose length dictate what kinds of pipes you can clean, but the water and debris tanks dictate the actual physical footprint, frame strength, and engineering layout of the truck.

**Q: For a municipal customer running a mixed sewer, storm, and catch basin program, how do you size the fleet?**

For a mixed-use city program, size the fleet around a standard 12-cubic-yard debris tank with a 1,000 to 1,500-gallon water tank configuration on a tandem-axle chassis. This provides the ideal weight-to-volume ratio to legally traverse municipal streets while handling both deep storm basins and long sewer runs. More broadly, sizing requires a data-driven approach that balances asset volume, regulatory mandates and equipment productivity. Because sanitary sewers, storm sewers, and catch basins require different maintenance cadences, sizing the fleet involves a five-step volume-and-production calculation coupled with strategic equipment allocation.
